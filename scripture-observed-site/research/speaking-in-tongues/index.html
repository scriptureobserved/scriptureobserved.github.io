What happens when we speak in tongues?

01 · Overview

The inquiry, before the answers

Speaking in tongues is described, practiced, interpreted, and studied in different ways. This project will map those perspectives without presenting unverified claims as settled fact.

The finished series will distinguish direct observations from proposed explanations, and scientific findings from theological interpretation.

  • What has actually been measured during glossolalia?
  • How do participants describe the experience?
  • What can linguistics identify in the vocal patterns?
  • How have scriptural texts and practices been understood over time?

02 · Brain

Attention, agency, and neural activity

This section will review peer-reviewed brain-imaging and cognitive research, including study design, sample size, comparison conditions, and limitations.

Research summary intentionally pending. No neuroscience conclusions will be published here until the original studies have been reviewed and cited.

03 · Body

Voice, breath, and physiology

This section will consider measurable bodily processes associated with vocal practice and reported experience.

Planned evidence: physiological measurements, experimental methods, and clearly labeled participant reports.

04 · Stress

Regulation and emotion

This section will examine research on stress, emotion, and repeated religious practice while avoiding causal claims that the available evidence cannot support.

Planned evidence: validated measures, comparison groups, study limitations, and potential confounding factors.

05 · Mental health

Wellbeing, distress, and careful distinctions

This section will use clinically responsible language and distinguish spiritual practices from symptoms that may require professional care.

Important: This project provides research information, not diagnosis or medical advice. Any future health-related claims will require high-quality sources and clear caveats.

06 · Language

Sound, pattern, and meaning

This section will define linguistic terms and examine what researchers can—and cannot—conclude from recordings and transcriptions.

Planned evidence: methods from phonetics, phonology, language acquisition, and sociolinguistics.
