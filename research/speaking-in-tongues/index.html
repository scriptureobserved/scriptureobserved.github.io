What happens when we speak in tongues?

A source-led inquiry into glossolalia—examining what has been measured, what remains uncertain, and how different fields frame the experience.

01 · Overview

What the evidence can—and cannot—tell us

Science can observe speech patterns, brain activity, physiology, reported experience, and social context. It cannot test whether an utterance has a divine source. The evidence so far is real but small: most direct studies use modest, culturally specific samples.

Observed

Measurable brain, speech, and stress-marker differences have been reported during or in association with glossolalia.

Not established

No study proves a supernatural cause, a medical benefit, a psychiatric disorder, or a natural language hidden in the speech.

Editorial position: report the measurements, show the limits, and keep scientific description separate from theological meaning.

02 · Brain

Activity changes—not a brain “switching off”

2006 preliminary SPECT study

5 participantsExperienced practitionersSinging comparison

Five women were scanned after religious singing and after glossolalia. Relative blood flow decreased in bilateral prefrontal regions, the left caudate, and left temporal pole; it increased in the left superior parietal region. The authors linked lower frontal perfusion cautiously to participants’ reported reduction in intentional control.

Limits: five participants, women only, a fixed order of conditions, one measurement window, and no correction for multiple comparisons. The study does not show that language areas “turn off,” nor does it identify the cause of the experience.

2020 structural MRI study

30 practitionersCross-sectionalNo non-practitioner control

Greater lifetime practice was associated with greater grey-matter volume in the left frontal pole and right middle frontal gyrus. The authors proposed a role for domain-general executive processes such as multitasking and interference suppression, rather than specialization in core language networks.

An association cannot tell us whether practice caused the structural difference. Pre-existing differences or other features of the community may contribute.

03 · Body

What has actually been measured

Direct, controlled evidence on breathing, heart rate, oxygen saturation, or vagal activity during glossolalia remains sparse. Claims that a particular breathing mechanism explains the experience—or produces a specific health outcome—currently run ahead of the evidence.

The strongest physiological work located for this review measured salivary cortisol and alpha-amylase across worship and non-worship days. Those findings belong in the stress section below; they should not be generalized to every bodily system.

Research gap: simultaneous voice, respiration, cardiovascular, and autonomic measurements with an active comparison condition.

04 · Stress

Arousal in worship, lower markers beyond worship

52 Apostolic PentecostalsObservationalSalivary biomarkers

Researchers collected saliva four times on a Sunday service day and the following non-service day. Indicators of glossolalia experience were associated with cortisol and alpha-amylase patterns. The authors interpreted the combined data as higher physiological stress or arousal during worship and lower stress and arousal beyond that setting among more experienced participants.

What this does not prove: worship included singing, movement, social interaction, and glossolalia. The study cannot isolate tongues as the cause, establish a therapeutic effect, or generalize beyond the sampled churches.

05 · Mental health

Context matters more than the sound alone

Research does not support treating culturally embedded glossolalia itself as schizophrenia or psychosis. In a 2020 comparison of 32 glossolalists, 32 matched controls, and 32 people with schizophrenia, the glossolalia group showed a different mental-state attribution profile from the schizophrenia group; none of the glossolalists had a diagnosed mental disorder.

Earlier experimental and survey studies likewise found no elevation on the mental-health measures they used. But clinical case samples show that tongue-like speech can also occur alongside mania or psychosis. Those clinical findings cannot be generalized to ordinary worshippers, and ordinary worship findings cannot rule out illness in an individual.

Practical distinction: distress, impaired daily functioning, dangerous behavior, persistent loss of control, and other symptoms warrant professional assessment regardless of religious interpretation. This page is research information, not diagnosis or medical advice.

06 · Language

Patterned speech without demonstrated semantics

Glossolalia can contain rhythm, intonation, repetition, and word- or sentence-like groupings. That makes it language-like, but a natural language also requires stable conventions that connect forms to meanings and organize them productively.

A modern acoustic corpus

11 German speakers15 recordings2024 publication

More frequent and predictable syllables were shorter—a statistical regularity also found in ordinary speech. About 60% of syllable types and 90% of syllable tokens also occurred in the comparison Southern German corpus, while some novel syllables did not follow German phonotactics. The result supports learned serial patterning, not a hidden translation.

Can it be learned?

In a 1986 experiment, about 20% of 60 participants produced fluent glossolalia-like speech after a brief example; after modeling, instruction, and practice, 70% of the trained group did so. This shows a learnable vocal component. It does not decide the religious meaning of a practitioner’s experience.

07 · Scripture

One term, different narrative settings

In Acts 2:4–11, the crowd hears the speakers in its own native languages; the passage uses both glōssa (tongue/language) and dialektos (language/dialect). In 1 Corinthians 12–14, a tongue may be unintelligible to the congregation and requires interpretation to build up the community.

  • Paul does not forbid tongues (1 Cor 14:39), but repeatedly prioritizes intelligibility and communal benefit.
  • Public use is limited to two or three speakers in turn, with an interpreter; without one, the speaker is to remain silent in the assembly (14:27–28).
  • Acts 10:44–48 and 19:1–7 connect tongues with reception of the Spirit but do not identify the speech as known human languages.

Interpretive question: whether Acts 2 and 1 Corinthians 14 describe the same kind of speech remains disputed. A 2025 linguistic analysis argues that the expression meant glossolalia in 1 Corinthians but acquired a foreign-language sense in Acts 2. That is a scholarly argument, not a settled doctrinal ruling.

08 · Timeline

Texts, revival, and research

  • First century: tongues appear in 1 Corinthians 12–14 and Acts 2, 10, and 19.
  • 1901: students at a Bible school in Topeka, Kansas, report speaking in tongues—one important starting point for modern Pentecostalism.
  • 1906: the Azusa Street revival led by William J. Seymour becomes a major international center of Pentecostal expansion.
  • 1906–1908: the mission publishes The Apostolic Faith, a primary source for the movement’s own testimony and theology.
  • 1960s: charismatic practice spreads within historic Protestant and Catholic churches.
  • 1970s–present: psychology, linguistics, anthropology, and neuroscience study the practice with competing models and small but growing datasets.

09 · Glossary

Working vocabulary

Glossolalia

Language-like vocalization without consistently recognizable semantic content or ordinary syntax in the samples studied. The term describes the observable speech; it does not explain its ultimate cause or religious meaning.

Xenoglossy / xenolalia

The claimed ability to speak an identifiable human language that the speaker has not learned. It should not be used as a synonym for glossolalia.

Interpretation

In 1 Corinthians 14, the practice that makes a tongue beneficial to the gathered community. The text presents it as a spiritual gift; it is not automatically equivalent to conventional linguistic translation.
